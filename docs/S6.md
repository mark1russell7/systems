# S6 · The requirements of the service abstraction

This file holds the requirements that `@mark1russell7/service` follows. They come from the first draft of S6, which jarvis keeps. That draft is of 2026-10-05, and it has 94 requirements. They come from the research rounds R1 to R5, and from two digests of an earlier health design by Mark.

This file moves the three groups that the runner follows: the lifecycle (A1 to A14), the health model (B1 to B19) and the configuration as data (E1 to E10). The other groups stay in the draft. Each group moves here with the part of the service that follows it. The status of S6 and its items are in [the roadmap](ROADMAP.md#s6--the-service-abstraction).

## The shape that the service follows

The draft proposes a shape and asks ten questions. Mark answered the first three on 2026-10-05, and Mark gave the other seven to Claude, decided as the draft recommends. The service follows these answers:

- **The declaration is the service.** A builder composes the service before it starts, with one method for each part. `describe()` gives the whole graph as data, and it starts nothing. Thus the declaration and the runtime cannot disagree.
- **A thin runtime.** The runtime takes each knob from the declaration, and it has no defaults of its own. A missing bound is infinite, not 0.
- **Readiness for each controller.** A controller declares its own dependencies. Thus a failed dependency fails only the routes that need it.
- **Two kinds of signal.** Active checks and the outcomes of live traffic are both evidence. A policy reads them and decides which one counts.
- **One timing shape.** Each policy is a debounce of polls or a window of time, and each one converts to the other. The algebra gets the detection bounds from either form.
- **A shutdown in declared phases.** Each owner adds its work to a phase with a cap, and the algebra checks the caps.
- **Machines as documents.** The machines of the runtime are `mark1russell7.machine/1` documents. The tests replay the transitions of the runtime against them.
- **Inequalities, not values.** The service states the inequalities that it needs from the platform. The scenario binds the values: the probes, the preStop delay and the grace period.
- **Wrappers, not patches.** A client is a wrapper of its library. A patch is only for a bug of the library, with a test that removes the patch after the fix.

## How to read the tables

The column "Source" names the documents of the research. These documents are in jarvis.

- `SYSTEMS §13.9` is a section of the design of the program.
- `R3 O13` is a rule of the ordering law of R3, and `R5 §8 C7` is a ruling of R5.
- `H1 §2.8` is a section of the first digest of the earlier design, and `H2 log 5` is a part of the log of the second digest.

The column "Ev." gives the kind of evidence:

- **M**: a measurement of the program.
- **D**: a document, or arithmetic on documented values.
- **N**: a design note of Mark, and the measurements of a cluster in it.
- **R**: a ruling of the program.

"Disagreement" marks two sources that do not agree, and it tells which way the evidence goes. The column "In the service" names the part of `@mark1russell7/service` 0.4.1 that meets the requirement. It also names the item of [the roadmap](ROADMAP.md) that adds a missing part.

## A · The lifecycle

| ID | Requirement | Source | Ev. | In the service |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A1 | The startup has a sequence: the configuration, the health server, the dependencies, the middleware, the transports, the metrics and the request servers. The service becomes ready last. The health server starts first, thus the probes answer while the dependencies connect. | H1 §2.7 | N | `run()` starts the health server, then each dependency, then each transport. The startup gate opens last. |
| A2 | The step that makes the service ready opens a startup gate in one direction. It does one warm-up sweep of each check, with a bound, and then it starts the pollers. A second use has no effect. The step does not occur after a server failed to start. | H1 §2.7, C7, H2 §2.7 | N | `start()` and `run()` do the warm-up sweep, and then start the loops. A part that does not start fails the startup. |
| A3 | The startup budget is longer than the connection of the dependencies. Only the connect timeouts put a bound on that connection. A connect into a black hole failed at 134.9 s in a measurement, and pg has no connect timeout. R3 finds O15 violated in 6 of 10 bindings. | R3 O15, R1 §4, H1 §3.9 | M, D | `startup(budget)`. The gate tells when the sweep outlasts the budget. `lint()` applies O15. |
| A4 | SIGINT, SIGQUIT and SIGTERM each ask for the exit one time. A signal during the startup gets its exit after the startup. The exit code is 1 after an error, and 0 for each other exit. The runner owns the handlers. Node as PID 1 without a handler ignores SIGTERM: 10.38 s and exit 137, against 0.34 s with `--init`. | H1 §2.11, H2 §3.1, R2 §11 | N, M | `run()` owns the three handlers. SYS-9 declares what a second signal does. SYS-16 gives the exit code of the work. Windows cannot give a child a SIGTERM that the child can catch, thus SYS-21 declares an IPC message that is that signal. |
| A5 | Library code does not end the process. An unhandled rejection ends it, thus background work catches its own failures. A loop that ended its process after more than 10 failures put a fleet in a crash loop during an outage of its broker. | H2 §3.1, log 6–7, 14 | N | Only the lifecycle ends the process, through its `ProcessPort`. A component gives parts and does not use `run()` (SYS-8). |
| A6 | The shutdown is a sequence of phases, each with a cap, and each owner adds its work. The phases are: fail readiness (2 s), stop intake (5 s), drain the transports with the signal live (the deadline plus 5 s), close the transports, stop the poller, abort the signal, tear down the dependencies in reverse sequence, and close the health server last. Disagreement: H1 aborted the signal after the teardown, because its consumers were in the dependencies. The later form of H2 drains the consumers first. | H2 §3.1, log 5, H1 §2.11 | N | The seven stages of `run()`, and `onPhase(name, work)`. The service has no default caps, thus each declaration gives them. SYS-10 gives profiles as data. |
| A7 | The caps come from the grace period less the preStop delay: 40 s for a grace of 60 s and a preStop of 20 s. The algebra checks them (O10, violated in 7 of 14, and O16). The caps that the notes set by hand add up to approximately 134 s. | H1 §3.10, P7 N7–N8, R3 O10, O16 | N, D | `lint()` applies O10 and O16 with the scenario of the platform. SYS-10 gets the caps from the grace and the preStop. |
| A8 | An HTTP server refuses new connections at SIGTERM. Thus a connection that comes late provably has no effect. The server closes the idle keep-alive sockets at once, and then each second. It fires a cooperative abort at G − 5 s, and not before G/2. It closes each connection by force at G. | H1 §2.11, C16, H2 §2.7, R2 §6 D7.g | N, D | The cooperative abort of the drain (`cooperativeAbortAt`). SYS-1 gives the HTTP server as a transport. |
| A9 | Each source of work has one transport contract: start, stop the intake, drain to a deadline without a throw, count the work in flight, close, and wait. A second close has no effect. The runner races the waits, and the default drain is 15 s. | H2 log 5, 8, H1 §2.11 | N | `TransportImpl`: `start`, `stopIntake`, `drain(deadline, signal)`, `inFlight` and `close`. Each phase races its cap in place of a wait. The declaration gives the drain cap. SYS-4 tests the contract. |
| A10 | A worker has a setup, an iteration and a teardown. Two iterations do not overlap. An abort can stop the sleep between them. The shutdown waits for the iteration in flight, and it does not count that iteration as a failure. The teardown occurs each time that the setup started. | H2 §3.1, log 7–8 | N | `workerLoop(knobs, options)` gives a worker as a `TransportImpl` (SYS-14). The iterations of a lane do not overlap, and `stopIntake` ends each sleep. The drain waits for the iteration in flight, and a cut is not a failure. The teardown occurs each time that the setup started. |
| A11 | A consumer drains in three steps: it cancels, it waits for the deliveries without an acknowledgment, and it closes (15 s). The transport acknowledges a success, and it puts a first failure back on the queue one time. Then it applies the redelivery policy of the queue, which the queue declares with no default. It acknowledges a poison message. | H2 §3.1, log 5, R1 DV-32 | N, D | `queueDrain(knobs, options)` gives a consumer as a `TransportImpl` (SYS-14). It stops its takes, waits for the jobs in flight, and closes the source. A success gets `ack`. The declared policy acts on a failure, and `redeliverOnce` gives this policy. |
| A12 | A failure that the shutdown causes becomes a 500, and the caller does not send it again. On a route that declares its idempotency, it becomes a 503 that the caller can send again. The definitions of these errors are data. The abort does not go into a driver that cannot cancel the work of the server. | H1 §2.11, §6.4, R1 DV-28, DV-37 | N, D | `life.request` answers a cut request with a retryable 503 on an idempotent route, and with a 500 on each other route. |
| A13 | The keep-alive of the server is a declared knob. While the service is not ready, each response except an upgrade has `Connection: close`. Disagreement: H1 sets 5 s, and Node closes at approximately 6 s (measured). The ordering law inverts that behind each hop with a longer idle timer (O01, O02, DV-08). Practice prefers 65 s. | H1 §2.11, §4.2, R1 §4, R3 §5 | N, M, D | `life.keepAlive()` is false while the service is not ready. SYS-1 makes the keep-alive of the HTTP server a declared knob. |
| A14 | The timers of the runner do not keep the process alive. The teardown closes each subscription and each poller. Otherwise a handle that stays open turns the drain into a SIGKILL. | H1 §2.9, H2 log 6 | N | The timers of `realClock` do not keep a Node process alive. `stop the poller` stops each check and each watchdog. The worker loop and the queue drain put each timer on the clock of the lifecycle, and their close stops it. The lifecycle holds its process from its start to its exit through its `ProcessPort`, thus the drain keeps its process. |

## B · The health model

| ID | Requirement | Source | Ev. | In the service |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| B1 | The process computes three verdicts. Started goes in one direction only. Ready is started, not in the drain, alive, and with no failed readiness sequence. Alive is no liveness window over its limit. Liveness passes from the start of the process. | H2 log 3–4, H1 §2.4 | N | `health.verdict()` gives started, draining, ready and alive. A liveness latch starts released. |
| B2 | Health is a chain of three state machines: the raw result, a gate for each check, and the counter of the kubelet with the propagation. The gate fails after 3 polls, recovers after 1, and starts tripped. The algebra computes each lag. | SYSTEMS §13.9, H1 §2.0 | N, R | A latch for each check that gates a policy. The declaration gives the counts. `failSpan` and `recoverSpan` give the lags. |
| B3 | Each check polls on its own loop. The probes answer from the cache in constant time, on a health port of their own. | H1 §2.2 | N | `health.probe(kind)` answers from the cache. SYS-2 gives a probe server on its own port, and a probe handler for an app port. |
| B4 | The outcome of a check is ok, error or indeterminate (nothing measured). Only ok passes. A timeout is an error, and a throw is an error with a flag. This outcome is not the same as the unknown outcome of an operation. | H1 §2.3, §6.1, R1 §1.1 | N, D | A check answers `"ok"`, `"error"` or `"indeterminate"`. An indeterminate poll moves no sequence. |
| B5 | Each poll makes a new measurement, because a memoized check once hid an outage for up to an hour. The runtime does not start a pending use again. Each later poll gives the same cause at once, and the sequence continues to count. | H1 §2.8, §2.12, P10, H2 log 5 | N | A pending use is not started again. Each later poll fails at once with the same cause. |
| B6 | The timeout of a check is long enough for a cold dial, and shorter than its interval: 4 s against 5 s (O12). The intervals have a jitter of ±20 %, on a schedule of fixed delay. | H1 §2.8–2.9, R3 O12 | N, D | Each check declares its interval, its jitter and its timeout. `lint()` applies O12. |
| B7 | Each step of a driver that a check exercises has a finite timeout, in one poll with its jitter. A check that hangs heals only when its driver heals: after 924.6 s in flight, and not at any time while idle. R3 finds O13 violated in 6 of 7. | SYSTEMS §13.9, H1 §3.5, R3 O13 | N, D | `lint()` applies O13. The Redis client binds its knobs for it. |
| B8 | The readiness latch fails after 3 and recovers after 1, and it starts tripped. A late registration takes the pod out until its first pass. A sequence or an interval of zero fails the decoding. | H1 §2.4, H2 log 1, R1 §6.5 | N, D | A readiness latch starts tripped, and so does a late check until its first pass. The builder refuses a zero interval or count. |
| B9 | Liveness windows count on the monotonic clock from the first failure of a sequence: 45 s for a wedge of the process, 5 min for a dependency as a last resort, and 0 for a check that times itself. Disagreement: H1 used the wall clock. H2 moved to the monotonic clock, because the real-time clock steps each 30 s in R4 and R5. | H1 §2.5, §2.9, H2 log 3, 7, R4 §9 | N, M | A window measures time on the monotonic clock from the first failure of a sequence. |
| B10 | A liveness failure fails readiness, and readiness detects before liveness (W8, O09). In kind without that coupling, each node refused 74–86 connections during a liveness restart. With the coupling, no node refused a connection. | SYSTEMS §13.9, H2 §2.5–2.6, R4 §5 | N, M | `couple({ from: "liveness", to: "readiness", effect: "fails" })`. No coupling applies without a declaration. `lint()` applies O09. |
| B11 | The model keeps apart the four ways that a pod stops: a deletion (preStop ≥ propagation, O04), a liveness restart (W8), a crash, and a stop for lack of memory (OOM). Disagreement: H2 expected a crashed pod to stay in its endpoints until the readiness threshold. R4 measured its removal in 0.28–0.33 s. | H2 §2.4, R4 §5, R3 O04 | N, M | The hold of `fail readiness` binds `readiness_hold@process`, and `lint()` applies O04. The service does not model the other three ways yet. |
| B12 | Workers stay out of readiness. Readiness does not depend on other internal services, because Kubernetes has no panic floor. The liveness window of a dependency is longer than its failover time (O14, violated in 5 of 9). Liveness does not examine readiness. | H1 §2.6, §3.7, P10, R3 O14 | N, D | Each check names the policy that it gates. `lint()` applies O14. |
| B13 | The gating posture is data on the registration. The gating flag is necessary and has no default: the law of REQUIRED-nullable, which the notes found independently. | H1 §2.1, §6.4, H2 log 1 | N, R | Each check declares `gates`, with no default. |
| B14 | Each check names the step that it exercises: dial, local state, round trip, checkout, progress, topology or latency. It also tells if it uses the connection of the application. Otherwise it cannot see DV-34. | SYSTEMS §13.9, H1 §2.12, §6.1 | N, R | Each check declares `step` and `ownConnection`. |
| B15 | The probe settings of the platform are declared knobs. Disagreement: H2 asks for a poll interval shorter than the probe period, but it probes each 2 s against a poll of 5 s. The clause of O11 for cached verdicts resolves it. | H2 log 4, §7.3, R3 O11 | N, D | The scenario of `lint()` binds the probes, and `lint()` applies O11. |
| B16 | Propagation is a declared distribution. For each node, it is p50 1.1 s, p99 8 s and p99.9 24 s. After a preStop of 20 s, the platform still routes approximately 28 % of deletions. Disagreement: H2 gives 15 s, and R3 binds the law at p99. R4 found that the metric makes the low end too large. | SYSTEMS §13.9, H2 §2.3, R3 §4.3, R4 §5 | N, M | The scenario of `lint()` binds the propagation. |
| B17 | Detection is computed, not configured. The latch trips in 8–18, 12–22 or 20–30 s, as a function of how the step fails. NotReady comes at 28–60 s, and a restart at 65–81 or 320–336 s. False trips change as p³, and removals as p⁷. | H1 §3, P8 | N | `failSpan` and `recoverSpan` give the bounds of each latch from its timing. |
| B18 | The service reports on itself. A report in `application/health+json` always answers 200, with signed sequences, and a 5xx for each dependency. It also has logs for each episode of failure, causes without secrets, counts for each outcome, histograms of durations that mark timeouts, and counts of transitions. | H1 §2.10, H2 log 2–3 | N | The `report` probe always answers 200, with the whole verdict. The transitions go to `record`. The logs, counts and histograms are not in the service yet. |
| B19 | A pair of a component and a measurement is a public contract. A nominated main check must be in its catalog, or the startup fails. The other checks of the catalog poll each 30 s and gate nothing. | H1 §2.1, §2.12, H2 log 6 | N | Not in the service yet. |

## The groups that stay in the draft

- **C · The clients (C1 to C15, and the rows for each system).** The Redis client of the service (`./redis`) follows C2, C3, C7, C8, CR2 and CR3. The next client is PostgreSQL, in the order of S1.
- **D · What the prober needs (D1 to D9).** The Redis client records the join of each connection at `ready` (D1, D2). The runtime gives its planned events to `record` (D7).
- **F · The tests and the chaos (F1 to F10).** `@mark1russell7/service/testing` follows F2 (`forceFail` and `quietServer`) and F3 (`contractCases`). The F6 matrix of the Redis client follows F6.
