A client opens a new connection to one target again and again. Each connect takes a local port from <Term name="ip_local_port_range" />. A closed connection can hold its port for a time, for example in <Term name="TIME_WAIT" />. When each port of a destination is held, a connect gets <Term name="EADDRNOTAVAIL" />. This page computes, in your browser, when the first connect fails and when the first port is free again.

## How to read the page

- **The headline** tells when the first connect fails, or why no connect fails.
- **The timeline** has two lanes. The search lane ends at the first error. The hold lane ends when the first held port comes back. Press **Play** to see the story.
- **The cards** below the timeline give each lane, with its knobs. Change a knob, and each time on the page changes.
- **An orange dot** is the median that a cell of the harness measured. It shows only when your settings are the settings of that cell. Its link opens the cell.

## The law of the first error

The client makes one connect each `gap`, to its `d` destinations in turn. Thus each destination gets one connect each `d · gap`. The search of one destination reaches `cap` ports, and each closed connection holds its port for a time `H`.

When `H` is more than `cap · d · gap`, the connect after the first `cap` connects of a destination finds no free port. That connect comes at `cap · d · gap`, and it gets EADDRNOTAVAIL at once. No SYN leaves. Thus the smallest gap with no error is `H / (cap · d)`.

With the defaults of Linux and one connect each 1 ms, connect 28,233 fails at 28.232 s. TIME_WAIT holds each port for 60 s, thus the first port comes back at 60 s.

## Where the numbers come from

The search for a port is the law of `portSearchSpan` and `portSearchShares` of `@mark1russell7/automata`. Linux reads the range in two passes. The first pass reads the ports of one parity, and the second pass reads the ports of the other parity.

- **An even range** gives each of its ports to each destination.
- **An odd range** can miss one port. Thus a search gets each port of the range, or one port less. For an odd range of 15 ports or fewer, the algebra gives the share of each count.

The time of a held port comes from the timers of the document of Linux TCP, `LINUX_TCP` of `@mark1russell7/machines`.

- **The client closes first.** Its socket waits in TIME_WAIT for 60 s. The timer fires on a tick of <Term name="HZ" />, thus the port can come back late. With <Term name="tcp_tw_reuse" /> 1, a connect takes a port in TIME_WAIT after `tcp_tw_reuse_delay`.
- **The target closes first.** The client waits in <Term name="LAST_ACK" /> until the ACK of its FIN arrives, one round trip later. When that ACK drops, the FIN goes again after one <Term name="RTO" />.
- **A reset** holds no port.

Each amount prints through `format` of `@mark1russell7/quantity`. The page does no timer arithmetic of its own.

The cells PR.1 to PR.8 measure each law on the runners of GitHub. Their results are on the [page of the port range cells](../../results/port-range/).

## Limits

- The page makes one connect each gap, at a fixed rate. A client that connects in bursts gets the error at the same count, but sooner.
- A destination is one address and one port of the target. Two ports of one target are two destinations, thus the client gets two times the connects.
- A NAT on the path keeps its own table of connections, with its own limit. This page does not show that table.
